## Plan item 2: custom grabbing and throwing

**Reuse review and decision.** Checked the installed packages and samples before writing anything. Meta XR Core 201 ships `OVRGrabber` and `OVRGrabbable` in `Scripts/Util/`: a complete grab, carry, and throw implementation that finds objects with a trigger volume, reparents or teleports what it holds, and takes release velocity from `OVRInput.GetLocalControllerVelocity`. Both were rejected. They are exactly the behaviour this project requires in its own code, and they read OVRInput for gameplay. The imported `ControllerModels` sample carries `OVRGrabbable` on 32 cubes with no grabber, so it offers no working behaviour either. `OVRInput.GetLocalControllerVelocity/AngularVelocity` was rejected for the same reason, and because the plan asks for our own bounded smoothing. Reused instead: Unity's sphere primitive and `SphereCollider` for the ball, the URP Lit shader for its material, the existing `GameplayInput` and `Hand` enum as the only input path, and the rig's `LeftHandAnchor`/`RightHandAnchor` for hand poses. A grep over `Assets/Scripts` and `Assets/Tests` finds no `OVR` or `Oculus` identifier, and the runtime assembly definition still does not reference `Oculus.VR`, so no OVR type can reach gameplay code.

**Change.** Four scripts in `Assets/Scripts/Interaction/`. `GrabSettings` is a ScriptableObject holding grab reach, hold speed and spin caps, the smoothing window and sample bound, throw scales, release caps, and a drop-on-tracking-loss switch; the asset is `Assets/Config/GrabSettings.asset`. `HandMotionEstimator` is a plain class with a bounded ring buffer of recent hand poses that reports the average linear and angular velocity across a time window. `Ball` requires a Rigidbody and a SphereCollider, keeps a registry of free balls, picks the nearest eligible one by distance to its surface, and admits exactly one holder; it turns gravity off while carried and restores the original setting on release. `HandGrabber` sits under each hand anchor and treats its own transform as the hand pose: it samples poses in `Update`, where the rig refreshes its anchors, resolves grab and release once per `FixedUpdate`, and carries the ball by setting Rigidbody linear and angular velocity, so a held ball still collides. `Assets/Prefabs/Basketball.prefab` is a 0.239 m sphere at 0.62 kg, continuous-dynamic and interpolated, with `Assets/Materials/Basketball.mat`; one instance sits at (0, 1, −9.55), within reach of the player start. At the developer's request a `Temporary Floor` cube was added, 30 m square with its top surface at exactly y = 0, so a released ball lands instead of falling away; its name says that plan item 3 replaces it. Two actions were added to `BasketballControls.inputactions` through the Input System API, `Tracked Left` and `Tracked Right` bound to `<XRController>{LeftHand|RightHand}/isTracked`, and assigned to the matching grabber. The three existing actions kept their ids and the scene's references still resolve. The importer's hidden back-compat reference per action means the asset now carries ten `InputActionReference` sub-assets for five actions; that is its normal output, not damage.

**Defect found and fixed: weak throws.** The first working version measured the throw inside the `FixedUpdate` that released the ball. A PlayMode check against a hand moving at a known 3.00 m/s returned 2.67 m/s, then 2.10 m/s on a repeat run. The cause is that poses sampled after the player let go, when the hand is already stopping, were averaged into the release window. `EndHold` now measures the throw at the moment the hold ends and `FixedUpdate` applies the stored value. The same check then returned 3.00 m/s, and 2.99 m/s on a later run, with the hand deliberately held still for three physics steps between the release and the assertion.

**Checks.** The code compiled without errors and all four types resolved in the `VRBasketball` assembly. `manage_scene validate` reported the scene clean with no missing scripts, and a wiring dump confirmed both grabbers parented to the correct hand anchor with hand, settings, `GameplayInput`, and the matching tracked action assigned, and the ball instance linked to its prefab with a 0.1195 m world radius. EditMode passed **8/8** in the Editor and again **8/8** in Unity batch mode with the Editor closed, which is recorded in the ignored `Logs/Verification/cli-manual/editmode.xml`; seven of those tests are new and cover the estimator's window bounding, sample bound, angular axis, and empty cases with exact numbers. PlayMode passed **7/7**, including a final run in a freshly launched Editor with a full domain reload. Those tests drive the hands directly, with no headset: a hand picks up a ball within reach, ignores one two metres away, only one hand can own a ball while the other is refused, a freed ball can be taken by the other hand, a still hand drops the ball with under 0.5 m/s of sideways motion and it then accelerates downward, a moving hand throws at hand speed, and disabling a hand returns its ball.

## Ball recall

**Request.** The developer asked for a way to get the ball back after a throw so repeated testing is easy, triggered by squeezing both hold controls, and noted it could grow into a visible effect later. They separately asked whether both hands can hold the ball at once.

**Gesture conflict and how it is resolved.** Both grips is also the natural gesture for a two-handed hold, so the two are told apart by state rather than by control: with empty hands both grips calls the ball back, and while a ball is held the gesture does nothing, leaving it free for a future two-handed hold. `TryRecall` refuses outright if any ball is held.

**Change.** `Assets/Scripts/Gameplay/BallRecall.cs` sits on `Player Rig` and reads the existing hold actions through `GameplayInput`, so recall stays on the Input System like every other control. It takes the loose ball furthest from the return point, turns its gravity off, and flies it in over a configurable `returnDuration`, default 0.6 s, on a smoothstep path driven by Rigidbody velocity at physics timing, the same way a carried ball is moved. The ball is delivered to `Ball Return Point`, a transform under `Player Rig` at chest height 0.45 m ahead, and waits there in the air so it can be picked straight back up; the first grab and release restore normal gravity through the existing `Ball` state. A hand that catches the ball mid-flight wins: the flight checks ownership every step and gives way. `Ball` gained `Active` and `FurthestFree` to support this. The gesture is edge-triggered, so holding both grips down calls the ball once rather than every frame.

**Checks.** PlayMode **21/21**, EditMode **9/9**. Five new tests cover delivery to the return point at rest, refusal while a hand holds a ball, catching a returning ball in flight, picking up a delivered ball straight away, and doing nothing when no return point is assigned.

**Revision: the ball comes back to the hands.** The developer asked for the ball to return to the player's hands and be grabbed on arrival, rather than to a fixed spot. `BallRecall` now takes a list of hands, assigned to both grabbers in the scene, and reads its destination fresh every physics step as the point between them, so the ball homes in and follows a hand that moves. No auto-grab code was needed: the call gesture is both grips, so the grips are still down when the ball arrives and the ordinary `TryGrab` takes it as soon as it is within reach, which also means one hand owns it and the other becomes support, leaving the player holding it with both hands. A ball that arrives untaken now has gravity restored and falls, instead of hanging in the air where a hand used to be. `Ball.RestoreFreeMotion` was added for that. The `Ball Return Point` transform is kept only as a fallback for when no hands are available.

**Limits.** There is no visual or audio effect on the return yet. The gesture has not been tried on the headset, and the proper reset and recovery rules still belong to plan item 5.

## Two-handed hold

**Request and decisions.** The developer asked whether both hands could hold the ball at once, with it sitting between the grips. They chose all three recommended behaviours: the remaining hand takes the ball over when the owning hand lets go, a two-handed throw is measured from the point between the hands, and the ball eases to that point over about 0.1 s rather than snapping.

**How it keeps one owner.** Plan item 2 requires that hands cannot both own the ball, so the second hand becomes a *support* rather than a second owner. `Ball` now has `Holder` and `Support`: `TryHold` gives the first hand ownership and a later hand the support slot, and refuses a third. `FindEligible` takes an `allowSecondHand` flag so a held ball is only offered to the other hand when the setting permits it. `ReleaseFrom` no longer drops the ball whenever the owner lets go: releasing the support just clears it, releasing the owner while a support is present promotes the support, and only a release with no other hand left applies the throw. `IsHeldBy` lets a hand tell that it still has hold whether it owns the ball or is steadying it.

**How it carries and throws.** Only the owning hand drives the Rigidbody; the other lends its pose. `HandGrabber` eases a `supportBlend` from 0 to 1 over `GrabSettings.TwoHandBlend`, and the carried point lerps from the single-hand hold offset to the midpoint of both hands, so taking hold with the second hand never pops the ball. The same blended point is what the motion estimator samples and what the lever arm is measured against, so a two-handed throw reports the motion of the point the ball actually rides on. `allowTwoHandedHold` and `twoHandBlend` are in the configuration asset, and turning the first off restores the old single-hand behaviour exactly.

**Checks.** EditMode **9/9**, PlayMode **25/25**, scene validated clean. The previous `OnlyOneHandCanHoldABall` test was rewritten as `OnlyOneHandEverOwnsTheBall`, since the second hand is now accepted as support while `Holder` stays put; it still asserts a single owner throughout and that both hands letting go clears everything. Four tests were added: the owning hand releasing hands over without throwing and without restoring gravity, the ball is only thrown once both hands let go, the ball rides at the midpoint when the second hand is moved, and the whole behaviour can be switched off from the settings asset.

**Limits.** The ball takes the owning hand's rotation rather than tracking the axis between the hands, so a two-handed hold does not yet twist with the wrists. None of this has been felt on a headset, and two-handed throw strength is untuned.

## Grab pose: twisting moved the ball, and recall landed off-centre

**Report.** From a live Play session the developer reported that turning the controllers moved the ball, which it should not, and that the ball no longer came back centred. They worked out the second cause themselves: the recall stopped as soon as the ball touched one controller instead of travelling all the way to the middle of both.

**Watching the session.** A 10 Hz recorder was installed through `EditorApplication.update` to log hand poses, ball position and the hand midpoint while the ball was held. It confirmed live tracked hands and a loose ball. No script was edited until Play had stopped, since recompiling forces a domain reload and would have ended the session.

**Cause 1, twisting moved the ball.** The carried point was `transform.position + transform.rotation * holdOffset`, so the grab offset was rotated by the hand and the ball orbited the wrist at that radius. Caught at full stretch the offset is large, so a wrist turn swung the ball a long way. The two-handed case was worse: the depth correction added for the previous fix read its depth from that same rotated point, so every twist changed the correction and the ball wandered in and out instead of sitting still. That regression came from the earlier `KeepDepth` change, which made centring depend on rotation when it had not before.

**Cause 2, recall landed off-centre.** Both grips are held during the call gesture, so the first hand whose reach the incoming ball entered grabbed it. The flight ended there, off to one side, with a large hold offset, and the other hand was usually too far away to become support, leaving a lopsided one-handed hold.

**Change.** Held with two hands the ball now rides on where the hands *are*, never on how they are turned: the target is the midpoint of the two hand positions plus a depth captured once, when the second hand arrives, along the player's flattened facing direction. Rotation no longer enters the two-handed target at all. For a single hand the offset still rotates, which is what a ball in one hand really does, but `SettleHold` now eases it in to `GrabSettings.HoldDistance`, 0.11 m by default, over `HoldSettle`, 0.15 s, so a ball caught at arm's length draws in to the hand and a wrist turn no longer swings it far. Because a two-handed ball does not swing with the wrist, the lever-arm contribution to the throw is scaled by `1 - supportBlend` so it fades out as the second hand takes hold. For the recall, `Ball.InTransit` reserves a returning ball so `FindEligible` ignores it, and on arrival `BallRecall.Deliver` puts it into every hand still asking, through the new `HandGrabber.TryTakeDelivered`, which skips the reach test but still refuses a hand that is not holding its grip or is untracked. The first hand takes ownership and the second steadies it, so a called ball lands centred between the controllers. Nothing takes it if both grips were released, and it falls.

**Checks.** EditMode **9/9**, PlayMode **28/28**. `TwoHandedHoldKeepsTheBallAtArmsLength` now also turns both controllers to sharp angles after the hold has settled and asserts the ball moves less than 3 cm. `AReturningBallIsNotSnatchedPartWayIn` puts a squeezing hand directly on the flight path and asserts the delivery is not cut short. `RecallWithBothGripsHeldArrivesCentredInBothHands` asserts a called ball arrives held by both hands and within 10 cm of their midpoint. ## Throw follows the whole arm swing, not the last flick

**Report.** Issue 3 from the developer's list: a throw only picks up the last flick of the controller before release, so a hand that snaps at the very end sends the ball that way. The throw should follow the whole arm trajectory.

**Cause.** The estimate measured the two *ends* of a 0.08 s window and nothing in between: `(newest - oldest) / elapsed`. Two things follow. A throw takes 0.2 s to 0.4 s of arm, so 0.08 s is only its last slice, and whatever the hand did in that slice decided the direction. And because only two poses were read, a single stray pose on either end moved the entire throw.

**Change.** `HandMotionEstimator.TryGetVelocity` now fits a constant-acceleration path through *every* pose in the window by least squares, and reports that path's velocity. A new `releaseBias` chooses where the fitted path is read: 0 reports the average velocity across the window, so only the arm decides the throw; 1 reports the velocity the path reaches at the instant of release, which keeps a still-accelerating swing at full strength but follows the end of the motion more closely. The two come from one fit — the fitted `b` is the release velocity and `b - c` the window average — so the bias slides along the same curve rather than blending two estimates. Turning is fitted the same way as travelling, by accumulating each step of rotation into a running vector so the path stays continuous past half a turn. Positions are fitted relative to the release so a hand far from the world origin does not lose its millimetres of travel. `Assets/Config/GrabSettings.asset` moves `velocityWindow` 0.08 to 0.2, `velocitySamples` 24 to 48 (0.2 s at 120 Hz with headroom), and `releaseBias` 0.5.

**Why a fit and not simply a longer window.** A longer window on the old two-point measure does reject the flick, but it reports the *average* speed of a swing that is still accelerating, so every throw goes out weak. Against a modelled 200 ms swing whose true release speed is 6.5 m/s, a plain 0.25 s average reads 3.4 m/s; the fit at bias 0.5 reads 6.1 m/s, against the old measure's 6.2 m/s. The fit rejects the flick without giving up the speed, which is what the bias exists to control.

**Measured on modelled swings.** These are synthetic hand paths, not recorded tracking, so they establish the mathematics and not the feel. On the deterministic path used by the EditMode test — 6 m/s straight ahead, with only the last 0.03 s also dropping at 3 m/s — the old measure ends 10.6 degrees off the way the arm swung and the new one 6.0 degrees, with the forward speed unchanged at 6.00 m/s. On a modelled 90 Hz swing peaking at 6.5 m/s, a 3 m/s flick over 30 ms moved the old measure 7.6 degrees and the new one 3.6; a 5 m/s flick over 40 ms moved them 18.4 and 10.8. For a stray pose, 1 cm of error on the newest pose moved the old measure 1.19 degrees and the fit 0.39; on the far edge of the old window, 1.19 degrees against 0.11.

**What the longer window costs, and why it stopped at 0.2 s.** A real throw curves, so reading more history aims the ball more at where the arm *was*. On a modelled swing arcing 40 degrees the fit lands 6.6 degrees off the release direction against the old measure's 5.1, and at 70 degrees 11.5 against 8.8. Widening the window to 0.3 s made that 9.5 and 16.7 degrees, which is why it was not taken further. The flick and the arc pull in opposite directions and 0.2 s with a bias of 0.5 is the balance point that was chosen; both are in the asset.

**Rejected: ignoring the last few milliseconds.** Skipping the final 0.03 s before the release event is the other common fix for this symptom, and it was measured rather than assumed. It erased the flick in the straight model, but that model's flick sits entirely inside the cut, so the result proves nothing. Its costs are real: a 40 degree arc went from 6.6 to 10.4 degrees off and a 70 degree arc from 11.5 to 18.2, and a 200 ms swing dropped from 6.1 to 5.2 m/s. It was left out rather than shipped as a second knob aimed at the same symptom. If the headset shows a jerk at the moment the grip is let go that the fit does not absorb, this is the next thing to try.

**Checks.** EditMode **12/12**, PlayMode **28/28**, clean compile with no new console errors. Three EditMode tests are new: a flicked swing asserts the fit ends closer to the arm than the old two-point measure by a wide margin while keeping the swing's speed; the bias is asserted to trade the swing against the last instant in the expected order; and a 1 cm stray pose is asserted to move the fit less than half as far as it moved the old measure. The acceleration test was rewritten — where it used to record that a longer window averages speed away, it now asserts the fit recovers a constant-acceleration release speed *exactly* at bias 1 and the window average at bias 0, which is the property that made the longer window affordable. The cofactor algebra was checked against an independent solver before it reached Unity. The PlayMode hands now move for 0.25 s rather than 0.15 s, because a window that is part stationary reads as a hand still accelerating and throws harder than it is moving; that is a harness fix, not a product one, and it is worth knowing the product behaves that way.

**Also.** Saving the settings asset through Unity wrote out `holdDistance`, `holdSettle`, `allowTwoHandedHold`, and `twoHandBlend`, which had been missing from the YAML and silently taking their values from the field initializers in code. The effective values are unchanged; they are now recorded in the asset, so changing a default in code will no longer quietly change the scene.

**Limits.** None of this has been felt on a headset, and feel is the whole point of the issue — the numbers above come from modelled swings, not from tracked poses, and the defaults are a starting point for the developer to tune. `releaseBias` is the knob: toward 1 if throws feel weak or unresponsive, toward 0 if they still wander. The arc cost above is a real regression for a strongly curving throw and has not been judged by hand. The angular fit sums world-space rotation vectors, which is exact about a fixed axis and an approximation when the axis itself turns, the same assumption the previous two-point version made. The other agent's Android manifest and App UI changes were present in the working tree throughout and were left untouched.

## Plan item 3: court and physical hoop

**Reuse checked first, and mostly rejected.** The MarpaStudio arena imported two commits ago is a real basketball pack — `BasketFloor`, `PlayField`, `Ring`, `Net`, `ScoreBoard` — and its court is modelled at true scale, `PlayField` being exactly 15.24 x 28.65 m. Its hoop is not. Fitting a circle to the `Ring.fbx` vertices puts the ring's hole at **0.840 m across against a regulation 0.457**, its top at **2.895 m against 3.048**, and its tube at 0.082 m against 0.020. `Net.fbx` agrees: a top radius of 0.452 is twice the 0.2286 it should be. Against a 0.239 m ball that hoop is 3.5 ball-widths wide where regulation is 1.9, so scoring through it would ask nothing of the shot, and the ring cannot be scaled down on its own because the board and the 3.81 m height come with it in one 672-triangle mesh. The developer then asked to keep assets simple and get the logic first, which settles it: the court is built from primitives, as the plan item says. The pack is still there for a later art pass, and the floor and a half-scale net remain usable when that happens. Synty has no court or hoop assets, only generic props and primitives.

**Dimensions are data, not scene nudges.** `Assets/Scripts/Court/CourtSettings.cs` holds the court and hoop measurements with regulation defaults, and `VR Basketball > Court > Rebuild Court` regenerates everything under a single `Court` root from it. The root sits at the ring's centre on the floor, at (0, 0, -5.4) where plan item 1's temporary forward target stood, so the established player facing is preserved; moving that one transform moves the whole court. Bounce and friction are deliberately **not** in the settings asset — they live in the physics materials in `Assets/Physics`, and the ball's mass and damping live on the prefab, so no value has two homes. `rimHeight` is the height of the *top* of the ring because that is how the rule is written, with the tube centre derived one tube radius below.

**The ring is capsules, not a mesh.** The collider is 24 capsules laid along the chords of the circle, each one's height set so its round caps land exactly on the division points, which means consecutive capsules share an end sphere and the loop has no gap. Capsules are primitives, so a fast ball gets exact contacts and continuous detection has something simple to sweep; a torus mesh collider that thin gives poor normals and invites the tunnelling the plan item warns about. The cost is that chords cut the corner: at 24 segments the hole is 2.04 mm tighter than nominal, which is 0.9% of its radius and smaller than the 10 mm tube itself. A matching torus mesh is generated to the same numbers and written to `Assets/Meshes/Ring.mesh`, so the visual and the collider cannot drift apart.

**Why the ball did not bounce.** The previous session recorded that it does not bounce at all and blamed the missing physics materials. That was half of it. The other half was the project's **bounce threshold of 2 m/s**: PhysX discards restitution below that, and a ball dropped from any sensible height lands under it, so no material value could have produced a bounce. It is now 0.5. With that fixed, the ball surface carries bounciness 0.85 and each court surface multiplies it down — floor 1.0, backboard 0.8, ring 0.55 — so the feel is tuned on the ball in one place.

**Rolling.** A Unity sphere has no rolling resistance, which is why the developer watched a ball roll fifteen metres off the floor edge. `RollingResistance` applies the standard resisting torque while the ball is touching something and nothing while it is in flight, because raising the Rigidbody's angular damping instead would eat the backspin off a shot. It is clamped so it can never brake past a standstill and spin the ball back up, and it stands down while a hand is carrying the ball. The coefficient is 0.12, which is well above the 0.02 a real ball on hardwood shows; at a realistic value the ball still rolls about thirty metres, so this is a gameplay number and a feel knob, not a physical one.

**Run-off behind the hoop.** The first build ran the floor out exactly to the baseline, which left the post 0.47 m from a 2.5 m wall and put a flat slab across the whole background of the shooting view. A shooter judges depth against that background. `baselineRunOff`, 2.5 m, now carries the floor past the baseline and the hoop stands clear against the sky.

**Checks.** EditMode **19/19**, PlayMode **33/33**, scene validated clean and saved. Seven new EditMode tests cover the ring geometry and, more usefully, the numbers actually shipped: the settings asset and the ball prefab are loaded and asserted against each other, so a hoop the ball cannot fit through fails the build rather than waiting for a playtest. Five new PlayMode tests run real physics — a 1.8 m drop rebounds into a 0.9-1.6 m band, a ball fired at the floor at 60 m/s (1.2 m per physics step, six times the floor's thickness) never gets through, a ball dropped down the middle of the ring reaches the floor without being thrown sideways, one dropped on the metal is thrown outwards, and rolling resistance is asserted against an identical ball without it. The saved scene's own colliders were then checked directly with casts rather than trusting the test harness: the board face sits at z = -5.021 as the dimensions say, a ball-sized sphere cast down the ring's centre reaches the floor, the same cast over the metal stops on a segment at exactly 3.048 m, and twelve casts spread around the inside of the hole are all clear.

**Two things the tests caught.** `ABallDroppedOnTheRingIsTurnedAside` first failed with the ball having moved 0.00006 m. The ring was not broken — a ball dropped dead on the crown of a horizontal tube *balances* there, which is evidence the collider is solid, and the assertion was simply the wrong expectation. The drop is now offset 2 cm onto the outer half of the tube, where the contact normal has somewhere to push. Separately, `RollingResistance` had been added to the prefab and the scene ball *before* its default was raised from 0.02 to 0.12, so both had the stale value serialised while the PlayMode test, which builds the component at runtime, read the new one and passed. This is the same trap the throw-tuning entry recorded for `GrabSettings`; both were written to 0.12 and the settings asset was force-saved so every field is on disk rather than taking its value from a code initialiser.

**Limits.** None of this has been felt on a headset, and the numbers that matter most — bounce, rim liveliness, rolling — are feel, not arithmetic. The ball starts on the floor 0.45 m in front of the player, so the first pickup means reaching down; the both-grips recall covers it after that, but a proper ball start belongs with plan item 5. The walls are still a temporary box at 2.5 m, and a ball that clears them is unrecoverable until item 5 adds recovery. There is no net, no court markings, and no scoreboard. The backboard is a plain box with no inner rectangle, and the ring is rigid, so there is no breakaway give and no net drag to slow a made shot. Nothing here detects a basket; that is item 4.

## Ball recall: intangible on the way in

**Why.** The developer asked for the recalled ball to drop its collider for the trip, in case something is standing in the path. It is a real failure, not a hypothetical: the flight is driven by setting the Rigidbody's velocity toward a moving target each step, so a solid ball that meets the backboard, the rim, or a wall is simply held against it while the target runs on without it, and the call ends with the ball nowhere near the hands. A control run of the new wall test with the change backed out measures exactly that — the ball finishes **6.62 m** short of the hands, stopped dead.

**The switch lives on the ball, not on the caller.** `Ball.InTransit` was an auto-property; it is now a property that suspends the ball's colliders when it is set and restores them when it is cleared. Every path that ends a trip already clears it — delivery into the hands, `Finish` for each give-up reason, and `BallRecall.OnDisable` — so there is no new path to keep in step and no way for a caller to leave a ball permanently intangible by forgetting a call. `Ball.OnDisable` now clears it too, so a ball switched off mid-flight cannot come back enabled and able to fall through the floor. Only the colliders this actually switched off are switched back on, so one disabled for its own reasons is not turned on by a trip that had nothing to do with it, and children are included because the ball's art may bring colliders of its own later.

**A cancelled call also left the ball hanging.** Found while testing the cancel path rather than reported: `TryRecall` turns gravity off for the trip, and `OnDisable` finished with `restoreGravity` false, so switching the caller off mid-flight left a free ball floating in mid-air indefinitely. It now passes `true`, which is what the other give-up path already did. `RestoreFreeMotion` ignores a held ball, so the fix cannot disturb one a hand has taken.

**Checks.** EditMode **19/19**, PlayMode **37/37**, clean compile with no console errors. Four new PlayMode tests: a ball called through a 1 m wall with continuous detection on, so it would be swept into the wall rather than tunnelling past it, still arrives within 0.1 m of the hands; the collider is asserted off at the moment of the call, still off part way in, and on again on arrival; a call cancelled by disabling the caller leaves the ball solid *and* falling; and a ball deactivated mid-flight keeps its collider. The wall test was run with the change backed out and fails as quoted above, so it is not passing vacuously.

**Limits.** Not felt on a headset. The ball turns solid again the instant it is delivered, wherever the hands are: if the player calls it with their hands inside the rim or a wall, PhysX will push it out on the next step. Nothing lifts the collider for a ball a hand is *carrying*, which is unchanged and intended. The scene was not touched — this is script-only, and the behaviour reaches the prefab through code rather than any serialised value. There is still no explicit player-facing cancel gesture; the cancel paths are the caller being disabled, the ball being deactivated, and the hands disappearing.

## Plan item 4: scoring and feedback

**Reuse checked first.** Nothing installed detects a basket, so the question was only what to build the rule and the readout out of. Four candidates were looked at. Unity's own answer is a **trigger volume**, and it is rejected on two counts below rather than on taste. The **MarpaStudio `ScoreBoard.prefab`** is one renderer, one material, one submesh, 206 triangles, 14 x 13 x 13.6 m in its own bounds: an arena-scale hanging prop with its face baked into a single albedo atlas, so there is no per-digit surface to drive and it cannot show a live score without new UVs or a new texture. It stays available as background scenery. **Synty's `Generic_Decal_Number_0..9`** is the real candidate — ten ready digit textures at 512 x 1024, one per numeral, which three quads could swap between. They were left out because they are a *decal* shader carrying mesh depth and view bias, angle fade, and a Built-In queue-control flag, meant for projecting onto Synty props rather than lighting a flat panel in URP; because there is no blank among them for suppressing a leading zero; and because ten 512 x 1024 textures against three flat colours is a poor trade for three digits. They are the obvious upgrade when there is an art pass. **TextMeshPro** ships inside `com.unity.ugui` 2.0, but its essential resources are not in the project — there is no `TMP Settings.asset` — so using it means importing a font atlas and standing up a canvas to draw three numerals, next to a court already built from boxes.

**A basket is a crossing, not an overlap.** `BasketDetector` takes the ball's position in the hoop's local space once per physics step and reports the step on which the path between two samples crossed the ring's plane downwards, inside the hole. This is the whole reason for not using a trigger. A shot drops more than a tenth of a metre per physics step, so a trigger thin enough to sit inside a 20 mm ring is one a fast ball passes straight through, and Unity's continuous detection does not rescue trigger events. And "is it inside" is a question a ball resting in a volume answers yes to for as long as it sits there, which is exactly the duplicate-score case the plan item asks about; a crossing happens once by construction. The detector has no scene, physics, or input behind it, so all ten of its rules are EditMode tests.

**Two samples, not one position.** The crossing point is interpolated between the sample above the plane and the sample below it, and it is that point that has to be inside the hole. Either end of a step can be well clear of the ring while the path between them went down the middle, and the reverse: a ball that dropped past the outside and swung back under the ring is *below the middle of the hole* on the next sample, which a check on the current position alone would count. Both directions are tests.

**Why the pass radius is generous and that is not sloppy.** It is set to the ring's inside radius, 0.2286 m, from the same settings asset the ring is built from. A ball that fits through has its centre within 0.109 m of the axis at the plane; a ball outside the ring cannot have its centre nearer than 0.368 m, because it has to clear the tube. The annulus between those two is unreachable, so any crossing inside 0.2286 m is a ball that went through the hole, and there is no precision to be had inside a gap nothing can occupy. The margin is what absorbs the error in interpolating a step.

**Arming is what stops one shot counting twice.** A downward crossing only counts if the ball has been at least 0.12 m clear above the ring since the last basket, and scoring disarms it. A ball rattling across the plane at the rim, or bouncing about under it, never gets back up there. A ball that only reached the underside of the ring was never a shot. A held ball is deliberately still watched, so pushing one down through the ring by hand is a dunk and counts.

**The recall would have awarded free baskets.** `BallRecall` flies a loose ball to the hands in a straight line with its colliders lifted, through anything in the way — including the hoop. The sensor skips and clears any ball whose `InTransit` is set. This is not hypothetical: the PlayMode test for it was run with the guard backed out and the recalled ball scores **2 points** as it is carried down past the ring, against 0 with the guard in.

**The readout is boxes.** `SevenSegment` holds which bars are lit for each numeral and where each bar sits; `Scoreboard` points each bar at a lit or unlit material. Three digits, twenty-one boxes, no font, no canvas, nothing imported — the same primitives the court is built from, and a shape that reads across a court at a glance, which is what a gym board is for. Leading zeros blank, so 7 reads as 7; a score past 999 holds at all nines rather than wrapping to something smaller than was actually scored. Feedback is the lit bars turning green, the board swelling 8% for 0.7 s, and a chime. The chime is generated in code rather than imported: there is no audio in the project, a basket that makes no sound is hard to judge on a headset, and this leaves no binary in the repository for something a real sound will replace.

**The first board shipped brown.** The lit bars were a URP Lit material with `_EmissionColor` set and `EnableKeyword("_EMISSION")` called before `AssetDatabase.CreateAsset`. The keyword does not survive being written out — the saved asset has an empty keyword list and `IsKeywordEnabled("_EMISSION")` is false — so the bars rendered as their dark base colour lit by the scene, and from the player's position the score was a dull brown smudge. Found by photographing it rather than by any test. The lit bars are now URP Unlit at the colour asked for, which needs no keyword, renders the same whatever the lighting does, is what a display is meant to look like, and costs the headset less than lighting a bar whose brightness was never meant to come from the scene.

**Where it lives.** `CourtBuilder` now builds the sensor onto the ring and the readout onto the backboard, and wires both to a `ScoreKeeper` on a `Scoring` root that it finds rather than replaces, so a keeper the developer has tuned survives a rebuild that destroys the hoop under it. The sensor's plane *is* the ring's transform, so moving or turning the hoop moves the rule with it; only the hole radius is read from `CourtSettings`, and the arming height stays on the component so that number has one home. The readout and its bars have their static flags cleared, because something that swells must not be batched into the scenery and something that changes material must not bake its light into it.

**Checks.** EditMode **38/38**, PlayMode **43/43**, clean compile, no console errors, scene rebuilt and saved. Twenty EditMode tests are new and the setup smoke test is deleted, as the plan item asks: ten on the rule, six on the readout, four on counting. Six PlayMode tests are new. Four run real physics against a ring built from `CourtLayout` — a shot through scores once and stays scored while the ball bounces underneath for five seconds, a shot onto the metal that is thrown clear scores nothing, a ball carried down through the ring by the game scores nothing, and two shots score twice. Two load the saved scene and run it: one asserts the sensor, keeper and board exist and are wired to each other with all twenty-one bars assigned, the other drops the scene's own ball down the scene's own hoop — positioned from the sensor's transform, so it cannot drift from where the court actually is — and asserts the score moves once. The scene tests ignore log failures for their duration, because the scene brings Meta XR Core's rig with it and that complains when it starts with no headset attached; the assertions themselves are unaffected.

**What was checked by looking.** The board was photographed in Play mode from the player's standing position, 4.6 m from the hoop: the score reads clearly in orange above the backboard. The flash was not photographed — the Editor crashed during an attempt to hold it open long enough to capture — so it was verified instead by reading back which material each bar points to and what the board's scale is: amber and 1.000 before, `ScoreDigitFlash` and 1.080 on the basket, amber and 1.000 again after a reset, which also confirms a reset is not celebrated as a basket. Nothing was lost to the crash; the scene had been saved beforehand and both suites were re-run against it afterwards.

**Limits.** None of this has been felt on a headset, and the two things that matter most there — whether the score is readable while shooting, and whether the chime lands at the right moment — are judgements a device makes, not a test. The chime has not been heard on a device at all. A basket is worth 2 points by default and there is no three-point line, no shot clock, and no net, so a made shot drops through silently and without drag. A ball thrown up through the hoop from underneath rises clear of the ring, which re-arms it, so its fall back through counts; in the real rules that is a violation, and it was left as the simpler reading of "a downward passage through the hoop". The board holds at 999. Nothing resets the score yet, and `ScoreKeeper.ResetScore` exists and is tested but is wired to no control — that belongs with plan item 5, which also has to decide whether a reset keeps the score. The scoreboard has no collider, so a ball thrown over the backboard passes through it.

## Plan item 5: reset

**The audit changed the scope, and the developer's instinct was right.** Asked to start item 5, the first finding was that most of it was already standing. `BallRecall` fetches a loose ball from *any* distance — `Ball.FurthestFree` does not care where it is, so a ball that cleared a wall is still recovered. *"Recovery cannot award a duplicate basket"* was already met and already proven by item 4's `InTransit` guard and its control run. *"Old ownership cannot persist"* was already structural: `Ball.OnDisable` clears holder, support and transit, and `HandGrabber.FixedUpdate` drops its reference when `held.IsHeldBy(this)` goes false. `ScoreKeeper.ResetScore()` and `BasketSensor.Clear()` were written during item 4 for exactly this and were already tested. What was actually missing was smaller: **the reset button did nothing**. `GameplayInput.ResetPressed` fires on right B and had zero subscribers anywhere in `Assets/`, so the README documented a dead control.

**Descoped on the developer's instruction.** Automatic out-of-bounds recovery was not built. The player recovers the ball with the recall gesture whenever they want to, and the temporary bounds walls stay. Item 5's *"recover balls that leave the playable area"* is therefore **deliberately unmet and deferred**, not quietly satisfied.

**Score on reset, decided and documented.** A tap of B puts the ball back and keeps the score, so a fumble costs nothing. Holding B for a second also clears the score to 0, which is how a fresh session starts. Timing the hold in code rather than adding a Hold interaction to the `.inputactions` asset keeps a tap meaning what it already meant; `GameplayInput` gained `IsResetting`, one expression mirroring the existing `IsHolding`.

**Taking the ball off a fist that is still closed.** The obvious move is to ask the hand to let go, and it is wrong: `HandGrabber.EndHold()` measures the throw the player is in the middle of and the release happens on the *next* physics step, so the ball would be launched at speed from wherever the reset had just set it down. Instead the ball stops being the hand's — `Ball.ForceRelease()` clears holder and support and restores gravity — and each hand notices on its next step through the branch that already exists for a ball taken from it. No hand is asked for anything and no throw is ever measured. A test moves the hand hard for twelve physics steps before resetting, so there is a real throw waiting to be applied, and asserts the ball ends under 1 m/s.

**A control run caught the new test passing for the wrong reason.** The reset clears every hoop's pending shot, because a reset drags the ball across the court in one jump and that jump can run down through a ring. Backing `BasketSensor.Clear()` out and re-running was supposed to make `ResetCarryingTheBallPastTheRingDoesNotScore` fail — **it passed**. The fixture's ball start sat 4 m from the hoop, so the teleport path crossed the ring's plane 0.59 m off the axis, well outside a 0.2286 m hole, and the guard was never reached. The fixture now puts the ball start *directly below the ring*, which the real scene's start is not, because that is the only arrangement in which the question arises at all. Re-running the control then failed as it should: **2 points awarded** for pressing reset, against 0 with the guard in. The guard is defensive for the shipped geometry — with the ball start 3.9 m in front of the player the crossing lands 0.65 m off-axis — and it is kept because a teleport is not a shot regardless of where the start happens to be today.

**What was built.** `Ball.ForceRelease()`, `GameplayInput.IsResetting`, and `Assets/Scripts/Gameplay/BallReset.cs` on the Player Rig beside `BallRecall`. `Restore()` and `Restore(Ball)` are public and shared: transit ended first so the ball gets its colliders back, then ownership cleared, then velocities zeroed and the ball moved. Both the Rigidbody's position and the transform are set, because the Rigidbody is what physics reads and the transform is what everything else reads until the next step. A `Ball Start` transform sits at rig-local (0, 0.12, 0.7) — on the floor, 0.7 m in front of the player and clear of the hands, so a reset visibly puts the ball down rather than dropping it back into a grip that is still closed.

**Checks.** EditMode **38/38**, PlayMode **49/49**, clean compile, no console errors, scene saved. Six new PlayMode tests: a reset mid-hold clears ownership and the hand lets go; a reset after a hard hand swing does not launch the ball; a ball in mid-flight comes back and arrives at rest; a reset dragging the ball down through the ring scores nothing; a reset keeps the score while clearing it stays a separate action; and a shot still scores *after* a reset, which proves the sensor was made to forget rather than switched off. The control run above is the evidence that the fourth of those is not vacuous.

**Limits.** Not felt on a headset — in particular nobody has judged whether 1 second is the right hold, whether the ball landing 0.7 m ahead on the floor is a comfortable place to pick it up, or whether a tap that keeps the score is what the hand expects. `ScoreKeeper.ResetScore` is now reachable, but there is still no on-screen confirmation that a long press cleared the score beyond the board changing. No automatic out-of-bounds recovery, as above. The reset restores *every* ball, which is right for one ball and would want thinking about with several. Nothing resets the player's position or the court; only the balls and, on a long press, the score.

## Dribbling on X

**Request and decision.** The developer asked for a dribble effect while holding **X**. Read as: with the ball in hand, holding X bounces it off the floor and back into the hand for as long as the control is down. X was free — the left controller's `primaryButton` had no binding — and the grip still means hold, so nothing already in the player's hands changed meaning. Nothing installed does this: Meta XR Core supplies the rig and controller models and no ball behaviour, and dribbling is ball motion, which `CLAUDE.md` keeps in project code regardless. What was reused is the project's own carry: `HandGrabber` already drives a held ball to a target point by Rigidbody velocity every physics step, so the dribble is that same carry with the target moved down to the floor and back, and the ball keeps colliding all the way.

**Timing kept away from physics.** `DribbleMotion` is a plain class with no ball, hand, or Unity object in it: it advances a phase, reports how far down the ball should be as a fraction of the way to the floor, and says which step lands. The path is a fall from rest at the hand and the same fall run backwards, so the ball is slowest where the hand takes it back and fastest at the floor. Two decisions are in there rather than in the hand. The floor is always met on a step of its own — the phase stops at the landing and the overshoot is carried to the next step — so a bounce lands visibly whatever the step rate; and the time spent waiting is given back on the way up, so 92 bounces in a row stay within one step of their due time instead of drifting late. A bounce under way always finishes: a ball in the air cannot be called back, so releasing X ends the dribble in the hand rather than snatching the ball up from the floor.

**How far to fall is asked of the scene, not assumed.** Each step a sphere cast the size of the ball goes straight down from where the hand holds it, and the distance it travels before touching is the drop. The floor's height is never hard-coded, the ball follows a hand that moves rather than returning to where the bounce began, and with nothing within `dribbleReach` underneath — held out past the court edge, or up at head height — there is nothing to bounce off and the ball stays in the hand. Hits at zero distance are skipped, which is the ball itself and anything it is already touching beside the hand.

**Letting go means two different things.** Release X and the bounce finishes in the hand. Release the **grip** mid-bounce and the ball is not in the hand at all, so `MeasureThrow` hands back the ball's own velocity instead of the hand's swing: the ball carries on the way it was going. Measuring the swing there would fire the ball off from a metre below the hand with a lever arm the wrist never earned. To shoot out of a dribble the player lets go of X first. A second hand taking hold ends the dribble where the ball is, because a ball held in two hands is not being dribbled.

**Changes.** `Assets/Scripts/Interaction/DribbleMotion.cs` (new), the dribble branch in `HandGrabber` (`BeginDribble`/`EndDribble`, `IsDribbling`, the drop applied in `CarryHeld`, the floor cast, the release branch), `dribblePeriod` 0.5 s and `dribbleReach` 2 m in `GrabSettings`, and `Dribble` in `GameplayInput` beside hold and reset. The action and its `<XRController>{LeftHand}/primaryButton` binding were added to `Assets/Input/BasketballControls.inputactions` through the Input System's own API and reimported rather than hand-edited, and the Player Rig's reference was set and the scene saved through Unity: one line of scene diff.

**Checks.** EditMode **45/45**, PlayMode **55/55**, clean compile, no console errors, scene saved. Seven new EditMode tests cover the bounce shape, the landing step, the rhythm over 92 bounces, and the finish in the hand. Six new PlayMode tests run it against real physics with a floor: the ball reaches within 4 cm of the floor from a hand 1.2 m up and comes back, stopping leaves it in the hand for longer than another bounce would take, a hand with nothing underneath keeps the ball still, letting go of the grip mid-bounce frees the ball at the speed it already had while the hand never moved, a second hand ends the dribble, and asking to dribble with empty hands does nothing until a ball arrives. One PlayMode run reported a failure on an *existing* test, `SettingsCapTheThrowSpeed`: the stack is Unity's own UIElements text job calling `Object.name` off the main thread while the Editor redrew, nothing in this project's code was in it, and it did not recur on the run that passed 55/55.

**Limits.** Not felt on a headset. Nobody has judged whether 0.5 s per bounce is a dribble or a bob, whether the ball should be pushed down rather than dropped, or whether the ball ends up where the hand expects it. The X binding follows the proven pattern of the B binding but has not been pressed on a device. There is no sound and no haptic on the bounce, so a dribble is currently silent and felt only through the eyes — the obvious next step, and `DribbleMotion` reports the landing step so something can be hung on it. Catching the dribble with the other hand is not a crossover: it becomes a two-handed hold and the dribble ends. Dribbling with two hands is not possible, deliberately. A dribble against a steep surface bounces off it, because the cast reports whatever is under the hand.


## Dressing the court and hoop from the arena pack

**Request and scope.** The developer asked to improve the look of the arena from three packs already in the project, explicitly "don't go crazy", and to be asked before any asset was replaced or a VFX added. Agreed scope after asking: the court floor and the hoop visual, the arena left as a simple box, the walls untouched, and VFX deferred to a later pass. Seating, stands, ceiling and props were declined.

**Reuse first, and one wrong assumption corrected.** `Assets/MarpaStudio` is a basketball arena pack and the only one of the three that fits: its `PlayField` is **15.24 m** across, exactly the project's `courtWidth`, so its markings land at true scale without being resized. Synty PolygonGeneric is URP-ready but stylised and carries no arena pieces, and mixing it with the pack's PBR look was rejected rather than attempted. This entry corrects a claim made during the session: the pack's materials were first reported as Built-in RP and magenta under URP, on the basis that its shader GUID `933532a4…` is the Standard shader. In this project that GUID resolves to `Packages/com.unity.render-pipelines.universal/Shaders/Lit.shader`, and all 36 materials in the pack's sample scene are already URP/Lit and supported. No conversion was done and the bundled 168 MB `ArenaURP.unitypackage` was **not** imported, so the repository carries nothing new from it.

**The floor.** `CourtBuilder` now generates `Assets/Meshes/CourtSurface.mesh`, a single mesh of two quads laid over the floor slab with the pack's `PlayField` material. The playing area is mapped out of the pack's atlas rather than stretched to fit — the baseline sits on the texture's baseline and the far edge falls wherever this court's 15 m reaches along a real 28.65 m one — so the key, the free-throw circle and the arc come out at the size a shooter judges distance by. Behind the baseline there is no court to draw and the pack has no plain boards outside its lines, so the run-off borrows the clean band between the centre circle and the top of the arc. The first attempt put the pack's `BasketFloor` material on the floor slab instead and produced a stretched blue apron behind the hoop; it was replaced rather than kept. Two quads in one mesh with one material, because this is a single draw call on a headset either way. The mesh carries no collider: the floor slab underneath is still what the ball bounces on, and its physics material is untouched.

**The hoop, and a decision that contradicts an earlier one.** Measured off the pack's mesh rather than guessed: rim inner radius **0.4205 m**, tube **0.0198 m**, rim top **2.8683 m**, glass **1.70 × 1.12 × 0.02 m** with its bottom at **2.6443 m**, stanchion centre **1.9292 m** behind the rim. The pack's hoop set is internally about **1.9× oversized** against real basketball — its own ball is 0.466 m where a real one is 0.24 m — while its court is regulation. That mismatch was put to the developer with three options: keep the project's geometry and only improve its materials, adopt the pack's dimensions wholesale, or graft the pack's board and stanchion onto the project's rim. **The developer chose to adopt the pack's dimensions.** `CourtSettings` was updated through Unity to the eight measured figures, and `CourtBuilder` now instantiates the pack's `Ring` prefab placed by its rim rather than its origin — turned to face the player, then shifted so the point its rim is modelled around lands on the ring the court builds — with the generated torus, the backboard box and the post reduced to colliders. The pack's `Net` is hung from the ring, scaled by the rim it was modelled around so it still meets a rim of other dimensions.

**This overturns a decision recorded in plan item 3, and the tests say so.** `TheShippedHoopLetsTheShippedBallThrough` holds the hole between 1.7 and 2.8 ball-widths, and its comment names this exact hoop: "the arena hoop rejected in plan item 3 was 3.5 times the ball across, which asks nothing of a shot." The hoop is now **3.49 ball-widths**, and the test fails on that bound. `TheShippedRingSitsAtAPlayableHeightAndTheRegulationDistanceFromTheBoard` also fails because the pack's board-to-ring gap is **0.1371 m** against the 0.15 m the rule fixes. Neither test was modified. The bound is a deliberate decision and the entry above says it is the thing to argue with rather than route around, so the failures are left standing for the developer to rule on.

**Changes.** `Assets/Editor/Automation/CourtBuilder.cs` (the court surface and its mesh, the arena hoop and net, `Invisible`, a shared `SaveMesh` that `SaveRingMesh` now also uses, and the post aligned to the pack's stanchion), `Assets/Config/CourtSettings.asset` (eight hoop dimensions, saved through Unity), `Assets/Meshes/CourtSurface.mesh` (new), `Assets/Meshes/Ring.mesh` and `Assets/Scenes/Gameplay.unity` (rebuilt and saved). The developer's uncommitted BoxCollider on the scoreboard `Panel` was about to be destroyed by the first rebuild — everything under the `Court` root is replaced wholesale — so it was baked into `BuildScoreboard` with the backboard's physics material before any rebuild ran, and survives now. Two pack materials, `SecondFloorEntrance.mat` and `TopFloorBarricade.mat`, show as modified: Unity filled in a `_MainTex` reference on import, nothing in this work touched them.

**Checks.** Geometry verified numerically against the mesh rather than by eye: visual rim centre 2.8449 m against a collider ring at 2.8485 m, a **3.6 mm** difference, and inner radius 0.4205 against 0.4167, a **3.7 mm** difference in the safe direction since the capsule loop is fractionally tighter than the steel drawn around it. The backboard collider matches the glass exactly on both axes, y 2.6443–3.7643 and z 0.5576–0.5776. Backboard, column and arm carry no renderer. EditMode **43 passed, 2 failed**, both named above and both a direct consequence of the chosen dimensions. PlayMode was **not run**. No new console errors.

**Limits.** Nothing here has been seen in a headset; every judgement above is a Play-mode screenshot and a measurement. The ball is now **0.239 m through an 0.841 m hole**, 28% of the rim's diameter where a real ball is about 52%, so it looks undersized against the new hoop and scoring is substantially easier than the 2.65 ball-widths the previous entry recorded. The pack's own 0.466 m ball would bring the ratio to 1.80 and back inside the test's bound, but the ball has tuned mass, radius and throw feel and was not touched. The pack's `Net` is a 9,336-vertex mesh, unmeasured on a device. The pack's textures are 2048² with albedo, normal, metallic, AO and height each and **no Android override**, which is heavy for Quest 3 and was left alone. The walls are still flat grey boxes by the developer's choice, and the VFX pass — ball trail, a hoop effect on a score, and a light effect on ball collisions — was agreed but not started.
